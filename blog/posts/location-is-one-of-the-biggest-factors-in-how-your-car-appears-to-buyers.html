Location Is One of the Biggest Factors in How Your Car Appears to Buyers. The Wrong Spot Distorts Pristine Paint While the Right One Can Emphasize the Car’s Best Features.

Michael J. Kelley Photographer
October 27, 2026 Automotive

What is that stain on the floor mat? It’s a lens flare. Why is the paint discolored on the left side of the hood? It’s a sunspot reflection. Is your mirror cracked? It’s a background reflection of a pole. Are those paint discolorations and hood scratches? It’s sun filtering through tree leaves. Is that the car’s antenna? No, a parking lot light sticking out the top.

Cars are like mirrors. They reflect the environment around them: the bushes and the fence, the garbage cans by the garage, the house behind it, and if you’re standing close enough, the photographer too.

Photographic anomalies can also be easily mistaken for the car’s condition. And the two surest ways to mitigate anomalies, distractions, and reflections are good light and a great location.

A lot of times, when I talk about location with an owner, they think “background:” the Golden Gate Bridge, Half Dome at sunrise with the car in the foreground, or epic mountains, like the Tetons rising out of clouds just behind the car. Although those images are great glam shots, in auction photography, location is not about the background. In glam shots the background is another spectacle, a thing in itself, with the car, rather than an environment whose only purpose is to enhance the car and not distract from it.

Auction photography is about whether a bidder, looking at your car on a screen, can see the paint and the shape of the body, or just the neighborhood and trees reflected in it.

Light and location are the most consequential choices on an outdoor car shoot. When you do those two things intentionally, I’d say they control more than 80 percent of how a set turns out. On good shoots, you get to make both those choices.

What Makes the Best Location for Car Photography?

There’s a spot in Marin I go to. It’s always empty, never crowded, even on weekends. I can go there on a weekend morning, shoot a car, and be out of there in less than two hours. Some mornings I’ve shot a couple of cars back to back.

The first Corvette I ever shot, a black 1962, I shot there at sunrise.

I crouched down level with the car, and the hills in the distance became the dark part of the bottom of the body. As the body curved up toward the sky, the reflection got lighter and lighter, until it reached the top, where all the light was hitting. The landscape formed a gradient on the body of the car, from the darkest part at the bottom to the brightest at the top, and that gradient emphasized its shape. Dark paint shows reflections most clearly, and on that car, at that spot, at that hour, I got completely clean reflections.

No stain on the mat. No discolored hood. Nothing coming out of the roof.

The spot has pavement, and the bushes and the fence around it sit far enough from the car, when you park it there, that they don’t reflect in the body. The nearest hills are maybe fifty to a hundred yards away, which is far enough that they just become the dark part at the bottom of the body, where that gradient starts.

A good reflection makes clean lines in the body of the car so you can see its design shape and the paint clearly. You just want consistent light on the paint if you can and a location that doesn’t distract from or distort the car.

I once found an open parking lot on top of a mountain in San Jose and was sure it would work, so we brought a Camaro there at sunrise. Nothing was blocking the sun, so it came straight onto the car, and the fence at the edge of the lot still showed up in the body. We had to keep maneuvering the car to find an angle without it.

It’s difficult, sometimes. An open spot in nature is my first pick, but it still has to have specific features.

Foundational Checklist for a Good Car Shoot Location:

  1. Enough room to work. I need to be able to stand fifteen to twenty feet in 360 degrees around the car. Can we park the car in one spot, leave it there the whole time, and I can move around it in my spiral process of circling closer and closer, moving from wide to detailed shots as I go? That’s when I can be fast and efficient.

  2. Environmental distractions reflecting in the car. If I’m twenty feet away from the car, whatever is behind me has to be low enough, like low grass or a low fence, so it doesn’t reflect in the body. And the car can’t be close to trees or bushes, because they all reflect in the body, and you’ll just see trees and bushes instead of paint condition.

  3. Tall obstructions that break clear views of the car. Street lights, parking lot lights, solar panels that stick up: when you’re moving around the car, they will stick up out of the car’s roof, and that’s a no-no. Is that an antenna, or is that a faraway light post? You don’t want trees coming out of the top of the car either. You want to see the roof clear from the background, so you can see its shape and where that shape ends.

  4. Even, clean light with no dapples or shadows. You can’t be under trees with sunlight filtering down through them, making shadows and distorting the body of the car. Any dark shadow and then bright sunlight crossing the vehicle, and you just cannot see the paint well.

  5. The sun’s position in reference to the landscape. I scout by season, with sun-tracking apps, and I take note of landscape features: the height of the nearby hills. I try to know exactly where the sun will come from and when it will come over those hills and blast the body and create sunspots.

  6. Clothes that reflect or blend into a car’s body. I once wore blue shoes and an orange jacket on a shoot, and those reflected in the car’s body no matter where I stood. I had to work extra hard to find angles to get me out of the shots. I now find it better to wear darker clothing and allow myself to blend into the darker reflections on the car or the wheels.

The Best Locations Provide an Environment that Enhances the Car’s Features Instead of Distorting Its Condition

The ideal location doesn’t take over the image, but creates an atmosphere, just a hint of what’s around the car, that enhances the car’s features as much as possible. The atmosphere can be rolling hills behind a car that pair with the car’s curved roofline, the hills in the background matching the shape of the roof, which is satisfying to look at.

It can be the opposite: I shot a Cybertruck, all hard angles and points, against similar rolling hills, and you get a contrast of lines and geometry. And there are abandoned buildings with boarded-up windows, where a car against that industrial, abandoned background draws the eye, and you’re like, where is that? I think your brain reads the contrast even if you’re not conscious of it.

There’s a road, a straight line with water on either side, where the background just disappears into water and sky.

Atmospheric environments are the difference between a sleek magazine ad shot and a dingy car catalog shot.

Plenty of photographers choose differently. A lot of influencers will take some hot car into a forest and shoot it with the entire forest reflected in the body, so you can’t see the paint at all. For them I think it’s a stylistic choice, a little artsy. It’s a fine choice if all you care about is stylization. It’s a poor choice for transactional photography like auctions. A spot without those reflections is just much harder to find.

What Is the Best Light for Car Photography?

Light is a whole other consideration that deserves its own post, but briefly, this is what I look for.

95% of the time, I like the sun at an angle to the car and the camera. In other words, I try not to have the car backlit by the sun, so I’m never shooting directly into the sun or silhouetting the car. There are rare moments when this can work, but for the most part I avoid it. I want whatever side of the car I’m shooting to be well lit with good light.

At sunrise, sometimes the pink of the sky shows up in the paint.

Golden hour is the best time to photograph cars on clear days.

That nice golden-hour light reflects on the car and that can look really beautiful as a reflection.

Unless it’s overcast, which can be really good for cars in late morning or late afternoon, the afternoon means sunspots and glare, and when they’re on the car, you can’t really tell whether the paint is damaged or clean. Glare on the body makes portions look white. And when the sun is too high, it creates a really dark shadow, and the tires are difficult to separate from that darkness.

So if I’m forced to shoot in poor light, I change my shooting angles to get the sunspot off the body, which limits the angles and perspectives I can get. They may not be the most flattering ones for that car, but you do what you can to get the best shots possible given the circumstances.

When you think of good light, think of diffusion (overcast), or the sun low in the sky (golden hour). The lower, depending on the location, the better. And shoot on the lit sides of the car, repositioning the car if necessary, to keep good light on the panels you’re shooting, and avoid silhouetting the car by shooting into the sun.

“We’re Doing It in My Driveway.” What Happens When You’re Stuck With Sucky Locations?

I don’t always get to pick. Before every auction shoot, I lead with a question for the owner: did you have a location in mind?

If they say the driveway, or a parking lot, I walk them through the auction brief. Bring a Trailer doesn’t want people walking through the shots or other cars in the frame, and I don’t want things coming out of the car’s roof, so if we do a parking lot, it has to be empty, with no light posts and no trees hanging over the car with sunlight filtering through.

I tell them I know locations that meet all of that and can recommend some in their area. Unless they can guarantee their spot meets those requirements, there’s a chance of rejection. I’ve never had a gallery rejected, but I’ve shot for a lot of owners who’ve had one rejected. It wastes a lot of time and results in a re-shoot.

When a car’s in a driveway, it just looks like it’s in someone’s driveway. And that can be as distracting as an epic background. And it also makes my job more difficult as far as movement around the vehicle, positioning, and keeping stuff out of the background, like garbage cans, etc.

Usually, once they hear all this, owners agree. Some don’t, and when someone insists on a location, there’s usually a good reason. Sometimes it’s an emotional one. Sometimes it’s stubbornness, or convenience. And sometimes, the smallest group, it’s an owner who has sold at auction before and already has a good spot picked out.

Owners are sure about their paint, too. I once shot a Porsche for an owner who builds custom 911 hot rods out of 1960s Porsches, and he told me the paint was a matte white, not really reflective, so we went out to a spot he picked.

It reflected.

He didn’t care. It was a race car with a storied history, banged up, with stickers and signatures and handwriting on the body, and he was an engine guy. What he cared about was what was under the hood. But the briefs I shoot ask for a clear view of the paint, and I’m always aware of the reflections, always trying to avoid them. A location can help with this or make it impossible.

You can’t listen to the owner all the time. You can’t trust them that a location is going to be good, or that even they know what the paint’s going to do in a photograph. You have to know that. And even if the color is matte, like even the matte G-Wagon I shot, it’s still going to reflect and still have distracting reflections in the paint, even if the car is a white matte and has markings on it. And you don’t want that.

It helps if the owner is flexible and trusts my locations, the ones I shoot in year round and know well, over any new location or their own recommendation. In the end, we both have to live with the result, and it’s better to live with the best environment possible than one that made good images impossible.

The Inevitable Worst-Case Car Shoot Locations and How to Deal With Them

The worst-case location for me is the before-mentioned driveway, usually, unless it’s large enough that it allows me to move and shoot the wide six-point shots. I’ve only done two driveways that were.

In a driveway shoot, you almost give up all your advantages as a photographer. The only thing you can control maybe is the time of day. I give up my whole shooting process. I give up speed. And often I give up the light, because an owner set on the driveway usually isn’t flexible about the time either, so the shoot lands at whatever time is convenient for him or her, and sometimes that’s the afternoon.

Recently I shot a 1973 Buick Electra in a driveway. We took it for a drive around the block, but I didn’t have the owner reposition it, because he was in his seventies or eighties and moving slow, and I thought it would take too long. I went over time anyway, because it’s just difficult to get all the shots in a driveway when you’re working every angle to keep the sun off the car.

In a driveway, I move the owner’s garbage cans out of the background, and I ask them to close the garage if it’s messy, so I’m not shooting into it. Sometimes I shoot at a more open aperture to blur whatever is behind the car.

I shot a 1941 Cadillac Series 62 Convertible, a museum piece that came out for parades, and we barely got it out of the driveway. We drove it half a block, shot the whole exterior in about fifteen minutes, keeping it running the entire time, and took it straight back.

It broke down at the driveway’s beginning edge near the sidewalk.

We pushed it down the long driveway, back into its parking spot, and I shot the interior there.

Not ideal.

Every driveway shoot I’ve done has been for a particular reason. The car is so old or so unreliable that the owner can’t drive it to a location. Or the owner doesn’t want to pay to have it trailered somewhere. Some dealerships will do that. A lot of owners, even collectors, don’t have a trailer or want to pay for one.

Sometimes it’s mileage preservation. The Cybertruck I shot against the rolling hills only had roughly seventy miles on it, and the owner only wanted to drive nearby. Understandable. I did end up convincing him to drive it twenty miles.

Other times the client is in a town new to me, and I don’t really have my go-to locations there, so I rely on them to know a good spot, and this is usually hit or miss.

I shot an amazing muscle car, a 1972 Oldsmobile Cutlass Supreme, in a full apartment parking lot, because the owner was in his eighties or nineties and didn’t want to drive it far, and I worked while cars came in and out and people walked by, and tried to do my best.

It kind of hurts, knowing those aren’t going in my portfolio. I remember these as the worst shoots, but looking back over the galleries now, the cars look great in the sets.

Sometimes owners are in their elder years, and you have to be compassionate and kind in those situations.

Sometimes the reason is emotional. I shot three cars, a 1968 Ferrari 365 GT 2+2, a 2011 Shelby Cobra CSX8000 50th Anniversary Edition, and a 1990 Ferrari Testarossa, on a sloped concrete entrance to a high school, because the owners’ late son’s name was on a plaque on the pavement below the cars. His charity had built that part of the school, and the auction proceeds were going to it.

They guaranteed me nobody would walk through the shots, and of course tons of high schoolers walked through the shots, checking out the cars, with lacrosse practice in the background, and I kept asking people to move out of the frame while the cars sat on concrete so sloped they didn’t even sit level. The location sucked. It was a long morning.

But there was a lot of emotional attachment to that spot, and that made it special for the owners, even though it wasn’t ideal for photography.

And I shot a Mercedes-Benz AMG GT R in a driveway because the family wouldn’t drive it. The car had belonged to their son, who had recently passed away, and his memory was in that car for them. There was a sacred sort of care and respect in how they treated it. It needed planks just to clear the dip at the end of the driveway, and they didn’t want to move it or touch it at all.

This is a car you’d normally take somewhere at sunrise or sunset, where the landscape reflects in the body at low light, emphasizing the sculpted design.

How can you convince someone in that situation?

You don’t. You just respect their situation and try to understand what they’re going through.

So I did my best in a normal driveway without a lot of room to work, and I opened the aperture to blur the residential background into shapes.

This driveway client did agree to timing.

So I shot the Mercedes at golden hour. As the sun set and the air cooled, the neighborhood quieted, and golden light streamed across the green hood. The car lit up against that neighborhood background. And I rushed through my shot list, making sure I got everything before the light faded.
