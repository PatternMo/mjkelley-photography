Industrial & Engineering Photography

Capturing a space's functionality and purpose in the most difficult camera conditions.

Industrial & Engineering Visual Services

At first glance one might look at an industrial space as dead and sterile. But like landscapes, industrial spaces are alive too, in a mechanical sense: vehicles in and out, lifts going up and down, people walking through, lights, noise.

How do you show that it's alive? How do you show its functionality and how people use it? When shooting engineering and industrial spaces, I start with these questions. I want to fully understand the subject.

Working throughout the San Francisco Bay Area, I photograph for:

  • Engineering firms
  • Mechanical and automated system installers
  • Architecture firms
  • General contractors
  • Developers and property owners

I use architecture techniques in the composite and in the lighting. I use luxury real estate framing for the one-point and two-point shots. In parking garages, I use car photography to place the car so it takes on desirable reflections on the platform and not distracting overexposed pieces of light. I use portraiture lighting for the mid-range and close-up shots of the panels and the things people interact with. Motion blur is an architecture and lifestyle technique. And I use landscape knowledge for thinking about the space, the sense of the space, and what light exists in there.

Figure out its function and how it is used, and you'll understand its nature. And understanding its nature informs how to shoot it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do you ask before an industrial shoot?
Is there natural light? Windows? Openings? These are some of the first questions I ask when discussing industrial and engineering assignments. I ask the client for any photographs they have from the build, and I correlate those with a sun study that maps the light coming into the building throughout the day. Then I try to time the shoot for the best light streaming in, if it has any. Most industrial spaces don't.
What if the space has no natural light?
Some spaces are like caves, and I use a significant amount of strobe lighting to bring out their character in crisp composites. In a cave, sometimes you have to create the light to show what's there.
How do you show the system working?
A mechanical system is alive when in use and still when at rest. So it's worth portraying both states. When shooting automated parking systems, for example, I show motion-blurred cars entering the garage or passing through the system. Gears and chains at rest. A finger about to press a control button. These are all choices discussed with the client to portray functionality and purpose.
How do you handle clutter in a working space?
I'm careful not to make these spaces unrealistically bright. You want the places that are dark to keep some of that shadow. Leaving aspects of the image dark, I can veil the parts of the space that don't flatter the photography or the place. Traffic cones packed into a corner, elements that don't contribute to the subject.
Can we use the photos for award entries, proposals, and our website? Can the architect, contractor, or manufacturer use them too?
Usage rights are standard in the photography industry. But I also offer split options, where each party has an opportunity to contribute to the overall cost of the shoots and images, and all get to use them. This usually makes it cheaper for each stakeholder. Otherwise, I do offer sets for sale to parties not involved with the original shoot contract.
What areas do you serve?
I serve the entire San Francisco Bay Area, including San Francisco, Oakland, Berkeley, San Jose, Palo Alto, and surrounding communities. Travel fees may apply for locations beyond 30 miles from Berkeley.
